Where the music comes from

The music in Case Detective is by Antonio Vivaldi (1678 to 1741). In the background you hear slow movements from his concertos and sonatas, one after another. When the Inspector takes out his violin, a short, lively piece plays. The music itself belongs to everyone now: it is in the public domain.

Nobody recorded these pieces for the game. The game holds the notes of each piece and plays them on your device, note by note, with recordings of a real violin, viola and cello (js/sampler.js and js/music.js). Each part of a piece has its own instrument. Until the recordings have arrived, and for plucked notes, small instruments made of code play instead.

What was changed: the MIDI file (the Mutopia editions) or the MusicXML file (the MuseScore scores) of each piece was turned into a short list of notes (the 18 files vivaldi-01.js to vivaldi-18.js in this folder), which also says which part, and so which instrument, plays each note. Each list is shared on the same terms as the edition it comes from: under the same license (the six pieces from The Four Seasons, CC BY-SA 3.0), or in the public domain when the edition is. Written trills, mordents and turns are played as the plain main note. The keyboard parts (harpsichord, organ and piano) are left out, and so are a few parts that only copy another part. The Siciliano’s last line is left out too, as its typesetter asks. In two concertos of Op. 3, a few notes under the lowest string of the viola or the cello are played an octave higher. Some slow movements are played a little slower than written, and most of the Inspector’s pieces stop where a phrase ends. README.md in this folder says what plays and why.

The violin, viola and cello

The violin, viola and cello you hear are made of recordings from TinySOL (“TinySOL: an audio dataset of isolated musical notes,” version 6.0, 2020), by Carmine-Emanuele Cella, Daniele Ghisi, Vincent Lostanlen, Fabien Lévy, Joshua Fineberg and Yan Maresz. Its notes come from Studio On Line, recorded at Ircam in Paris. TinySOL is shared on Zenodo under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license (CC BY 4.0).

What was changed: the game uses 32 of its violin notes (16 pitches from the violin’s lowest G up to a high E), 28 of its cello notes (14 pitches from the cello’s lowest C up to a high D-sharp) and 22 of its viola notes (11 pitches from the viola’s lowest C up to a high F-sharp), each played softly and a little louder. Each recording was trimmed to the start of the note and its first seconds, looped (so that a note can be held as long as the music asks), level-matched (so that all of them are equally loud) and re-encoded as MP3 in the files of the folders violin/, viola/ and cello/. As it plays, the game raises or lowers a recording to play the notes in between, inside those ranges by a semitone at most. The lists of the recordings used are in violin/CREDITS.md, viola/CREDITS.md and cello/CREDITS.md.
