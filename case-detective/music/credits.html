Where the music comes from

The violin in Case Detective plays the 24 Caprices for solo violin, Op. 1, by Niccolò Paganini (1782 to 1840). He wrote them between about 1802 and 1817, and they were first printed in 1820. The music itself belongs to everyone now: it is in the public domain.

Nobody recorded these pieces for the game. The game holds the notes of each piece and plays them on your device, note by note, with recordings of a real violin (js/sampler.js and js/music.js). Until those recordings have arrived, and for plucked notes, a small violin made of code plays instead.

In the background you hear slow passages of the caprices, played a little slower than written, and when the Inspector takes out his violin, a fast caprice plays.

The notes

Somebody has to type a score into a computer before a program can play it. The notes in this game were taken from these free editions:

  • Nos. 1, 2 and 3: typeset by Andrew Buck for the Mutopia Project, shared under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 3.0 license (CC BY-SA 3.0). Pages of the editions: No. 1, No. 2, No. 3.
  • Nos. 4 to 20 and 22: typeset by Musemeister on MuseScore and released into the public domain (CC0 1.0), taken from PDMX (“PDMX: A Large-Scale Public Domain MusicXML Dataset for Symbolic Music Processing,” by Phillip Long, Zachary Novack, Julian McAuley and Taylor Berg-Kirkpatrick, 2025), which is shared under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license (CC BY 4.0). Pages of the scores: No. 4, No. 5, No. 6, No. 7, No. 8, No. 9, No. 10, No. 11, No. 12, No. 13, No. 14, No. 15, No. 16, No. 17, No. 18, No. 19, No. 20, No. 22.
  • Nos. 23 and 24: typeset by Samuel Rummel for the Mutopia Project, shared under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 license (CC BY-SA 4.0). Pages of the editions: No. 23, No. 24.

What was changed: the MIDI file (Nos. 1, 2, 3, 23 and 24) or the MusicXML file (Nos. 4 to 20 and 22) of each edition was turned into a short list of notes (the files caprice-NN.js in this folder). Each list is shared on the same terms as the edition it comes from: under the same license, or in the public domain (CC0 1.0) when the edition is. The MIDI files of Nos. 1, 2 and 3 give no tempo, so a tempo was chosen from the tempo words of the score (Andante, Moderato, Sostenuto and Presto). The tempo of No. 19 was changed by the table of sources: its MusicXML file plays the return of the Allegro assai theme (the second ending, from measure 42) at the Minore’s 80 quarter notes a minute, about half the speed of the same music at measure 5, so there it gets the Allegro assai’s 150 again; the rest keeps the file’s tempo (Lento 70, Allegro assai 150, Minore 80).

No. 21 is not in the game yet.

The violin

The violin you hear is made of recordings from TinySOL (“TinySOL: an audio dataset of isolated musical notes,” version 6.0, 2020), by Carmine-Emanuele Cella, Daniele Ghisi, Vincent Lostanlen, Fabien Lévy, Joshua Fineberg and Yan Maresz. Its notes come from Studio On Line, recorded at Ircam in Paris. TinySOL is shared on Zenodo under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license (CC BY 4.0).

What was changed: the game uses 32 of its violin notes, 16 pitches from the violin’s lowest G up to a high E, each played softly and a little louder. Each recording was trimmed to the start of the note and its first seconds, looped (so that a note can be held as long as the music asks), level-matched (so that all of them are equally loud) and re-encoded as MP3 in three files of this folder (violin/violin-01.js to violin-03.js). As it plays, the game raises or lowers a recording to play the notes in between, inside that range by a semitone at most. The list of the recordings used is in violin/CREDITS.md.
